Project showcase

Flight Movement Analysis — Aircraft Tracks Around Leeds

Research question. How does aircraft traffic behave over time in the airspace around Leeds, and can the raw ADS-B position reports be reduced to a small number of interpretable descriptors of that traffic?

Dataset. 33,766 ADS-B position reports covering 2017/09/11–2017/09/16 from 805 distinct aircraft, distributed as course material for CISC7204.

Visualization.

What the visualization shows. Panel (a) plots all 33,766 position reports as raw points: they form a dense blob with no visible structure. Panel (b) draws the same points as polylines — one per aircraft, joined in time order — using the timestamp reconstructed from the separate date and time columns. The scatter turns into visible flight corridors, which is the evidence that ordering by the reconstructed timestamp recovers real paths rather than producing noise.

The second figure isolates a single continuous flight (192 reports over seven minutes). Reports from the same aircraft on different days are split into separate segments whenever the gap between them exceeds five minutes, so no line is ever drawn across a jump in time that was not actually flown.

Main finding. The airspace is dominated by high-altitude transit: 72.8 % of all reports sit above 20,000 ft, while only a small secondary cluster (1,000–5,000 ft) looks like terminal traffic, and each aircraft contributes a median of just 18 reports. The data therefore describes many brief overflights rather than a few flights tracked end to end.

Supporting findings.

  • The airspace carries 805 distinct aircraft, but observations are heavily skewed: median 18 reports per aircraft against a maximum of 564.
  • 72.8 % of all reports are above 20,000 ft, with a secondary cluster at 1,000–5,000 ft — a clear separation between overflights/cruise and terminal traffic.
  • Some sampled windows contain no reports at all, so coverage is intermittent rather than continuous.

One limitation. ADS-B reception depends on ground-station coverage and line of sight, so the file records what was received, not every aircraft that was flying. Every count above is therefore a lower bound, and no conclusion about total traffic volume should be drawn from it.
